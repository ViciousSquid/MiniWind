Title: The Lockwright's Patience
Author: O.

Not a manual for thieves. A manual for locksmiths, which is a different thing, though I grant you the difference is mostly in what you do afterwards.

A lock is a promise made of iron: this box will open for one key and no other. Like most promises it is only as good as the man who made it. A Millbrook lock is honest work, four pins and a good spring. A lock from the coast is pretty and lies. A lock on a barrow-chest is older than the Seven and may not have been made by men at all, and I would leave it be.

Every pin in a lock wants to fall. Your pick lifts it; your tension holds it. Too much tension and the pin binds and will not rise. Too little and it drops the moment you move on. The whole of the art is in that one wrist, and it cannot be taught in a book. It can only be told, and then practised, and then practised again.

Listen. A set pin speaks: a small click, felt more than heard. If you are hurrying you will not hear it, and if you do not hear it you will push past it, and if you push past it you will snap your pick, and that is the sound of the lesson being learned.

Carry more picks than you think you need. Every apprentice breaks a dozen in his first week, and the first week lasts longer than you would think.

Do not pick a lock with its owner watching. This is not a rule of the craft. It is a rule of having two good hands to practise the craft with.

If a lock will not open, it is telling you something. Perhaps it is telling you that you are not good enough yet. Perhaps it is telling you that whatever is inside would rather stay there. In my experience it is right about the second more often than you would like.
