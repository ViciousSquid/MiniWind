Title: The Chronicle of Miniwind
Author: Master Corwen, Reeve's Clerk of Millbrook

Being a short history of the Vale, set down for the instruction of the young.

The Vale of Miniwind lies between the mountain wall and the Emberpeaks, a bowl of good land some days' walk across, watered by the streams that run down from the high country into Mirrormere. It has been settled for longer than anyone has kept count, but the history the Vale itself tells begins with the coming of the Seven Families.

The Seven came from beyond the mountain wall in a year of famine, driving their cattle before them through the pass that is now called Kingsfall. They found the Vale empty of men but not of other things: wolves in Greywood, greenskins in the hills, and something in the lake that would not suffer a boat upon it until a bargain was struck. Of that bargain the folk of Reedhollow tell their own tales, and I will not repeat them here.


The Seven built their first hall where Millbrook now stands, beside the stream that turns the mill, and from that hall the Vale was ruled for three hundred years by a council of their heads. Each family took a part of the Vale for its own: the Ashes the north, the Fenns the lake shore, the Thorns the forest, the Coldharbours the eastern farms, and so on. Their names live on in the villages, though few of the families do.

The council fell in the Long Winter, when the snow lay on the Vale from harvest to midsummer and the people ate their seed corn and then their cattle and then their boots. It is said that the heads of the Seven argued so long over who should open their granaries that by the time they agreed there was nothing in the granaries to share. After the thaw, the folk of the Vale would have no more councils. They chose a reeve instead, one man to keep the peace and the stores, and they have chosen one every seven years since.


The watch was raised in the time of Reeve Halloran, after the brigands of Cutthroat Ridge burned Pennick Farm and carried off its harvest. Before that there was no law in the Vale beyond what a man could enforce with his own hands. Halloran raised forty men, armed them from the reeve's own purse, and hanged the brigand chief Black Jory at the crossroads that is still called Hollin's Cross after the gallows tree. The watch has kept the roads ever since, more or less, and the guards of Millbrook are its children.

Of the Emberpeaks, little need be said. They smoke in the north-east, and sometimes they rumble, and the snow on their tops is grey with ash in a bad year. There is an old shrine high on them, reached by a pass marked with cairns, where the firekeepers of the Seven tended a flame that was said never to go out. It went out, in the end, as all flames do. What became of the book of fire-lore they kept there, I do not know, though the blacksmiths of Millbrook will tell you stories about it if you buy them a drink.


As for the present day: the Vale is quieter than it was, and richer. Wheat grows in the south and the east, sheep graze the hills, and the trade road carries Millbrook's iron and Oakridge's grain as far as the passes. Travellers come and go. Brigands still haunt the ridges, and there are wolves in Greywood and worse things in the barrows, and every few years some fool goes down into the red hills of the south-west looking for a dragon. But the mill turns, and the watch keeps the roads, and the reeve keeps the stores, and the Vale endures.

Let the young who read this remember that it was not always so, and need not always be. The Vale was made by people who worked together through a hard winter, and lost when they did not. It is the same now.
