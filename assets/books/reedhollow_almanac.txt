Title: The Reedhollow Almanac
Author: Gammer Fenn, who has watched the sky for seventy years

Being the year as it turns on the lake shore, month by month, with what to sow and what to fear. Learned from my mother and hers, and corrected where they were wrong, which was seldom.

Deepwinter. The lake freezes from the reeds outward. Do not trust the ice until a goose will stand on it, and do not trust a goose. Mend nets. Tell the old stories, for the children will not sit still for them once the thaw comes.

Thawmonth. When the ice groans at night it is breaking, not haunted, whatever your grandfather told you. Pike come up into the shallows to spawn. Take them then, for they are stupid with love.

Seedmonth. Sow beans when the alder buds are the size of a mouse's ear. Sow barley when you can sit on the earth bare-arsed without complaint. If you will not test it so, you will not get barley, and that is your own affair.

Greenmonth. The lambs come down from the High Fold and the wolves come down after them. Keep the dogs in at night and the shepherds sober. A red sky over the Emberpeaks in Greenmonth means dry weather; a grey one means ash, and you must cover the well.

Highsun. Cut hay at Oakridge before the feast, not after. Every year someone says the weather will hold through the feast, and every year it does not, and every year it is a man from Thornfield who said it.

Harvest. Bring it in. Bring it all in. Leave one sheaf standing in the far corner of the last field, for the lake, and do not ask me why, for I do not know, only that the Fenns always did and the Fenns are still here.

Leaffall. Mirrormere rises. Move the boats up the bank and the hens up the loft. This is the month the drowned are said to walk; I have never seen one, but I have never gone looking, either, and I recommend the same to you.

Frostmonth. Kill the pig. Salt the pig. Thank the pig. Bank the fires and count the stores, and if the count is short, say so now and not in Deepwinter, when there is nobody left to buy from.

Some sayings, which are true more often than not:

A ring round the moon, rain by the noon.

When the herons stand on one leg, the frost is coming; when they stand on none, they have flown, and so should you.

Wind from Greywood, close the door. Wind from the lake, open it. Wind from the Emberpeaks, pray.

A full larder is the best almanac. This one is second.
