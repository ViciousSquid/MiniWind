Title: The Ember Wyrm of Whisperdeep
Author: Brother Aldric of the Wayfarer's Shrine

"Gold is a fire that never goes out. That is why the dragon sleeps on it."

— proverb of the Emberpeak miners

I was sent into the south-west in my fortieth year, by the order of my abbot, to learn the truth of a thing the shepherds of the Vale had talked about for as long as there had been shepherds: that in the red hills where the Vale gives way to sand there was a hole in the ground, and in the hole there was a dragon, and the dragon was older than the Vale itself.

My abbot did not believe in dragons. Neither, I will admit, did I. I had read the bestiaries, as every novice does, and I knew that the great wyrms of the old tales were held by the learned to be the shapes our grandfathers' grandfathers gave to the fires that came out of the Emberpeaks — burning rock and choking smoke, given claws and wings by the telling. I went north expecting to find a cave full of bats and a story full of holes, and to come home with a sermon on the foolishness of the credulous.

I did not come home with that sermon. I have written this instead, and I have written it plainly, without ornament, so that whoever reads it after me may judge for themselves whether I am a fool.


The cave is called Whisperdeep by the people who live near it, though there are not many of them, and fewer every year. The name comes from the sound the wind makes in the entrance on still days: a long low murmur, like many voices talking at a great distance. The shepherds say it is the voices of everyone the dragon has eaten. I record this as I was told it.

I came to the cave on the ninth day of my journey, having followed a goat track up from the dunes through thorn and scree. The entrance is a mouth in the side of a steep hill, framed by two great standing slabs of rock with a third laid over them, like a door. I took this at first for the work of men — some old tomb, perhaps — but when I came close I saw that the stones were not cut but split, and that the split faces were black and glassy, as though they had been melted and had cooled again.

There was no smell of beast. I had expected a reek. Instead there was a warm dry smell like a smithy at night, after the forge has been banked, and a faint taste in the air of hot iron.

I lit my lamp, said a prayer to the Wayfarer, and went in.


The first part of the cave is a winding passage, wide enough for a cart, its floor worn smooth. Not worn by water: there is no water in Whisperdeep, and that is one of the strange things about it, for every other cave in those hills drips and runs and has moss in its cracks. Whisperdeep is dry as an oven. The walls are warm to the touch. The deeper you go, the warmer they become.

Here and there in the passage I found bones. Sheep, mostly, and goats, and once the great curving skull of an aurochs, which no one has seen alive in the Vale for three hundred years. None of the bones were gnawed. They were cracked, and some of them were blackened, but none of them showed the marks of teeth. I thought about this a great deal afterward.

I also found, half buried in the grit of the floor, a sword. It was a long blade of an old pattern, with a hilt wrapped in wire that had once been gold and was now green. Its edge was notched in a dozen places and its point was gone, and the steel of the last hand's breadth of the blade had run like candle wax and set again in a drooping curl. I left it where it lay. I am not a man of the sword, but I knew a warning when I saw one.


After perhaps half an hour's walking the passage opened out, and my lamp could not find the walls.

I have tried many times since to describe that hall, and I have never done it well. It is very large. It is round, or nearly so, as though something had turned and turned in it over many years the way a dog turns in its bed, and worn it round. The roof is lost in the dark. The air is so warm that I took off my cloak and still sweated.

And the floor is gold.

I do not mean that there was gold upon the floor, though there was. I mean that the floor of that hall, as far as my lamp could reach, was gold: coins of every age and every kingdom, cups, chains, plates, crowns, the hilts of swords and the bosses of shields, all heaped and spilled and drifted together like leaves in an autumn wood. In places it lay knee deep. In places it had run together into lumps and sheets, as the sword in the passage had. It glowed in my lamplight with a dull warm light of its own, and I understood, standing there, why men had come to this place for a thousand years knowing what waited in it.

I did not touch it. I want that understood. I did not touch any of it.


He was lying in the middle of the hall, on the deepest part of the gold, and at first I did not see him at all, because he was so large that I took him for part of the cave.

I saw a ridge of rock, red-brown and rough, running away into the dark. I saw that it rose and fell, very slowly, like the swell of a sea. I saw that along the top of it ran a crest of something that flickered, red and orange and gold — not fire, exactly, but like fire, the way a fire's reflection on a wall is like fire. And then I saw the head.

It was resting on the gold, turned a little toward me, and it was bigger than a farm cart. It was green — that surprised me; I had always imagined dragons red — a deep old green like a pine wood in winter, covered in scales as small as a fingernail near the eyes and as large as a shield along the jaw. Long whiskers trailed from its lips across the gold, and a beard of that same flickering flame-stuff hung beneath its chin. Two horns swept back from its brow, brown and ridged like the horns of a ram, each as long as a man is tall. Its eyes were closed.

I stood very still. I am not ashamed to say that I wet myself, a little. I did not run, only because my legs would not carry me.

And the dragon opened its eyes.


They were yellow. Not gold, like the hoard; yellow, like a lamp behind horn, with pupils like the slot in a door. They looked at me, and I felt that look the way you feel heat on your face when you open an oven.

It did not move. I did not move. We regarded one another for what may have been a moment or may have been an hour. Then it spoke.

I will not try to describe its voice, except to say that I heard it less with my ears than with my ribs. It said, "You have not touched the gold."

"No," I said.

"Why not?"

I thought about this. I had a great many reasons, all of them good, and I found I could not remember any of them. So I said the truth, which was: "It is not mine."

The dragon was quiet for a long time. The flickering crest along its back rose and fell. Then it said, "No one has said that to me in four hundred years."


I stayed in the hall with the dragon for, I think, a day and a night. It is hard to say; there is no light in Whisperdeep but what you bring. We talked. I will not set down everything it said, because much of it I did not understand and some of it I did not want to, but I will set down what I can.

It told me its name, which I will not write, because it asked me not to. The people of the Vale call it Vharzul, which is not its name, but which it said it did not mind.

It told me that it had come to the Vale when the Emberpeaks were still burning — when the whole north-east was fire and ash, and the rivers ran hot — because it had been born in fire and could not abide the cold; and that when the mountains cooled and the snow came, it had gone south-west to the hot sands, and dug itself a home in the red hills, where the ground is never cold. It told me that it had slept through most of the history of the Vale, waking every hundred years or so to eat, and to see who had come to steal from it this time.

It told me that it did not want the gold. I asked it, then, why it kept it, and it laughed, which is a sound I hope never to hear again, and said that it did not keep the gold; the gold kept it. Every coin on that floor, it said, had been brought there by someone who came to take it away, and who had died, and whose gold had stayed. It was not a treasure. It was a graveyard.


"They come," the dragon said, "because they hear of the gold. They do not hear of the bones. Or they hear, and they think: those were weaker than I. And they come in with their swords and their spears and their spells, and they are brave, some of them, and clever, some of them, and it does not matter. I am older than their grandfathers' kingdoms. Their swords melt. Their spells go out like candles. And their gold lies down with the rest."

"Do you hate them?" I asked.

"No," it said. "I hated the first ones. Now I am only tired. Every hundred years I wake and there is someone at the door, with a sword in their hand and the gold in their eyes, and I must get up and do it again."

"Then why not leave?" I said. "Fly away. Find another mountain."

It turned its great head and looked at me with one yellow eye.

"Because," it said, "if I leave, they will come and take the gold, and go out into the Vale with it, and kill each other for it, and that will be worse. Here, at least, it stops."

I have thought about that answer every day since.


When I left, it let me go. I asked it, at the end, whether I should tell the people of the Vale what it had told me, so that they would stop coming. It said I could tell them whatever I liked, and they would come anyway. It said a fool with a sword does not read books. I said that I would write one anyway, in case a fool with a sword ever learned to read, and it laughed again, and closed its eyes, and I walked back up the long passage in the dark with my lamp guttering and the voices of the wind murmuring in the entrance.

I went past the melted sword without looking at it.

So here is my book. I have written down what I saw and what I was told. If you are reading this because you mean to go to Whisperdeep for the gold, I cannot stop you. Many better than me have tried and failed. But I will tell you three things that the dragon told me, which no one else will.

The first is that it is very, very old, and very large, and that it is not afraid of you.

The second is that it breathes fire, and that when it does, you will have a moment — a heartbeat, no more — in which you can see it gather the fire in its throat, and get out of the way. Most do not.

The third is that it is tired. And that if anyone ever came to Whisperdeep and did not want the gold, it would be glad to see them.

May the Wayfarer keep you on your road.
