Title: Weights, Measures and the Honest Pedlar
Author: Dunstan of the Road

Twenty years I have walked between Ashby and Brackenford with a pack on my back, and I have been cheated by every town in the Vale at least once. Here is what it taught me, so that you may be cheated a little less.

First: know what a thing is worth before you are asked to pay for it. Ask the price in three places. The first merchant will tell you what he hopes, the second what he needs, and the third what it is. Buy from the third, unless the third is me, in which case buy from me anyway, I have a family.

Second: the man who sells must want to sell more than you want to buy. Never let him see you want it. Pick it up, put it down, ask about something else. Come back to it as though you had forgotten it was there. This is called haggling and the merchants all know you are doing it, but they respect the effort, and the respect is worth a copper or two.

Third: a merchant buys what he can sell again. A smith does not want your poems, and an innkeeper does not want your scrap iron. Take each thing to the man who has a shelf for it, and you will be paid like a friend instead of turned away like a fool.

Fourth: a stolen thing is cheap to buy and dear to own. I have seen a man hanged at Hollin's Cross for a silver cup he paid two coppers for. The guards do not care how little you paid.

Fifth, and last: be pleasant. A smile costs nothing and the merchant who likes you will remember it, and next year, when times are hard, he will knock a penny off without being asked. That penny is the best bargain you will ever make.
