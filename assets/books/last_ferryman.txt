Title: The Last Ferryman of Mirrormere
Author: Edda Reedwife

"The lake keeps what it is given, and gives back what it is owed."

— a saying of the Reedhollow folk

In the years before the bridge at Coldharbour, when the only way across Mirrormere was by water, there was a ferryman named Tobiah Wenn. He was neither young nor old, neither handsome nor plain, and if you had passed him on the road to Millbrook you would not have looked at him twice. But on the lake he was something else. The fishermen of Reedhollow said he could read the water the way a priest reads a book, and that he had never once lost a passenger in thirty years of crossing.

His boat was a long, flat thing of black alder, older than he was, with a lantern hung from a crooked pole at the stern. He charged two coppers for a crossing and a silver penny after dark, and he would take anyone who paid: farmers with their geese, pilgrims bound for the Moonwell, soldiers of the watch, and once — so the story goes — a woman in grey who paid him in a coin no one in the Vale had ever seen, and who was not in the boat when it reached the far shore.

Tobiah did not speak of that crossing. When people asked him, he would only say that the lake had many passengers, and that not all of them were his.


I heard this story first from my grandmother, who heard it from hers, and I set it down now because the old crossing is gone and the people who remember it are going with it. Some of what follows I have from the parish book at Reedhollow, which recorded the drownings and the weddings side by side. Some of it I have from the songs. And some of it, I confess, I have from nowhere at all but the lake itself, which has a way of putting words in your mouth if you sit by it long enough on a still evening.

It began in the autumn of the wet year, the year the Emberpeaks smoked for a fortnight and the wheat at Oakridge rotted in the fields. The rains came early and did not stop, and Mirrormere rose until it lapped at the doorsteps of the lower houses in Reedhollow and the reeds stood up to their necks in black water.

That was the autumn the drowned began to come back.

The first to see one was a girl called Wren Aske, who was cutting rushes at the edge of the lake at dusk. She looked up and saw a man standing in the water a little way out, up to his waist, quite still. She called to him, thinking he had fallen from a boat, and he turned his head and looked at her, and she saw that his eyes were full of lake water, the way a cup is full, and that weed hung from his collar.


She knew him. He was Aldo Fenn, who had gone through the ice eleven winters before, when she was a baby. She had seen his name in the parish book.

Wren ran home and told her mother, and her mother beat her for telling lies, and then went down to the water herself with a lantern and came back white and silent and did not speak for the rest of the night.

Within a week, a dozen people in Reedhollow had seen them. They stood in the shallows at dusk, never moving, never speaking, watching the village. Some of them were known: Aldo Fenn, and old Mother Crane who had walked into the lake one spring for reasons no one ever learned, and two brothers lost in a storm when the elder Wenn — Tobiah's own father — was still the ferryman. Others no one recognised at all, in clothes of a cut no one had worn for a hundred years.

They did no harm. That was the strangest part, people said afterward. They did no harm at all. They only stood, and watched, and when the sun was gone they were gone with it, and the water where they had stood was smooth as glass.


The priest from Millbrook came and said prayers on the shore, and the drowned stood and listened, and were there again the next evening. A hedge-witch came from Greywood and burned bundles of rue and spoke to them in the old tongue, and they turned their heads to look at her and she went home and would not come back. The reeve sent to the watch at Ashby, and the watch sent two men with crossbows, who stood on the jetty all one evening and then went back to Ashby and reported that there was nothing in Reedhollow that a crossbow could mend.

All this while, Tobiah Wenn went on ferrying. He crossed at dawn and at noon and at dusk, as he always had, and the drowned did not trouble him. If anything, the fishermen said, they seemed to watch his boat more closely than anything else on the lake, turning their heads to follow the lantern as it went out across the water.

It was Wren Aske, in the end, who asked him about it. She was a bold girl, and since the evening she had seen Aldo Fenn she had become bolder, the way children sometimes do when they have been frightened past the point of fear. She went down to the jetty one morning as Tobiah was bailing out his boat and asked him straight out why the drowned had come back.

He did not answer for a long while. Then he said, "Because they have not been paid for."


"Paid for?" Wren said.

Tobiah set down his bailer and sat on the thwart of his boat and looked out at the lake, which was the colour of pewter that morning, with a mist lying on it like wool.

"My father told me," he said, "and his father told him. When the first folk came to the Vale, they could not cross the lake. Every boat they put on it went down. The lake would not have them. So they made a bargain. They said: give us passage, and we will give you passage in return. Every soul the lake takes, we will carry across. And the lake agreed."

"Carry across where?" said Wren.

Tobiah pointed. Far out on the water, where the mist was thickest, there was nothing to see. Mirrormere is not so wide that you cannot see the far shore on a clear day — the willows at Smugglers' Cove, the roof of Dunstan's camp — but that morning there was only mist, and something in the mist that might have been a darker line, like a shore, or might have been nothing.

"There is another shore," he said. "My father showed it to me when I was your age. You can only find it at dusk, when the light is going, and only if you have a passenger who belongs there. Every one of the drowned should have been rowed across. That was the bargain. My father did it, and his father. And I have not."


"Why not?" said Wren.

Tobiah was quiet again. Then he said, "Because I was afraid."

He told her then about the night of the woman in grey. She had come to the jetty just at dusk, and paid him with her strange coin, and he had rowed her out — not toward Smugglers' Cove, where the ferry always went, but where she pointed, out into the middle of the lake where the mist was gathering. He had rowed for what felt like an hour, though the lake is not an hour wide, and the lantern had burned blue, and the water had grown so still that the oars made no sound in it. And then he had seen the other shore: a long, low beach of pale stones, and beyond it a country of grey grass and grey trees under a sky with no sun in it, and on the beach a great crowd of people standing and waiting, and every one of them turned to look at him.

"I knew some of them," Tobiah said. "My mother was there. She had been dead nine years. She lifted her hand to me." He was quiet for a moment. "I turned the boat around. I rowed back as hard as I have ever rowed in my life. And when I got to the jetty, the woman in grey was gone, and I have never rowed out to that shore again."


"And since then," said Wren, "the drowned have had no one to carry them."

"Since then," said Tobiah, "they have been waiting in the water. Eleven years. And this autumn the lake has risen high enough that they can stand up and be seen."

Wren thought about this for a long time, sitting on the jetty with her feet over the water. Then she said, "Then you must take them."

Tobiah laughed — not a happy sound. "Child, I am not sure I can come back from that shore a second time. When I turned the boat, I felt it pulling at me. Like a hook in the chest. If I go there again, I think it will keep me."

"Then I will come with you," said Wren, "and hold the lantern, and make sure you turn the boat."

He tried to argue with her, of course. He told her it was no place for a child, that her mother would never allow it, that the drowned were not to be meddled with. But Wren had made up her mind, and in the end — this is the part of the story the songs make the most of — Tobiah Wenn agreed, because he was tired of being afraid, and because he could not stand to see his own mother standing in the shallows at dusk any longer, waiting for a boat that never came.


They went out on the evening of the first frost. The whole village came down to the shore to watch, though no one would come onto the jetty. The drowned were standing in the water as they always did, thirty or forty of them now, in a long ragged line from the reeds to the end of the jetty.

Tobiah lit the lantern and climbed into the boat, and Wren climbed in after him and took the lantern-pole in both hands. And then — the parish book records this, in the priest's own hand — the drowned began to move. One by one, they walked out of the shallows and along the water to the boat, and stepped into it, and sat down. The boat should have sunk under the weight of so many. It did not. It sat a little lower in the water, that was all, and the drowned sat in rows on the thwarts and the floorboards, wet and silent, with their hands in their laps, like any passengers waiting to be taken across.

Tobiah's mother was the last to step in. She sat down in the stern beside him and did not look at him, and he did not look at her, and he took up the oars.

The lantern burned blue as they left the jetty.


What happened on the lake that night, no one can say for certain. Wren told the story many times in her long life, and it was never quite the same twice, which my grandmother said was how you knew it was true: a lie is the same every time, because the liar has to remember it.

But in every telling there were these things. The mist came down so thick that she could not see the bow of the boat from the stern. The oars made no sound. The water grew so still that the lantern's reflection lay on it unbroken, like a second lantern hanging beneath them. And after a long time — she could never say how long — the boat touched stones, and she saw the pale beach and the grey country beyond it, and the great waiting crowd.

The drowned stood up and stepped out of the boat, one after another, onto the stones. Some of them looked back. Aldo Fenn did; he raised a hand to Wren, and she raised hers. Old Mother Crane did not look back at all, but walked straight up the beach into the grey grass as though she knew exactly where she was going and had been late getting there.

And Tobiah's mother stood up last, and put her hand on her son's shoulder, and said something to him that Wren did not hear. And then she stepped onto the stones and walked away into the crowd, and Tobiah sat with the oars in his hands and wept.


Then Wren felt it: the pull. She said it was like standing at the edge of a high place and feeling the ground lean toward you. The beach wanted them. The grey country wanted them. She saw Tobiah's hands loosen on the oars, and his eyes go to the crowd on the shore, searching it, and she knew that in a moment he would stand up and step out of the boat after his mother.

So she did what she had promised. She swung the lantern-pole hard and struck him across the shoulders with it, and shouted his name, and the lantern swung wildly and flared from blue to gold, the honest gold of an ordinary flame. And Tobiah blinked, and looked at her, and took up the oars, and turned the boat around.

They came back to the jetty at Reedhollow an hour before dawn. The whole village was still waiting on the shore, with fires lit. There was frost on the reeds and the lake was perfectly empty, from shore to shore, and it was never again seen to rise as high as it had that autumn.


Tobiah Wenn ferried for nine more years after that night, and every year on the evening of the first frost he rowed out into the middle of Mirrormere alone, with the lantern lit, and came back before dawn. He never said where he had been. When he died, he left the black alder boat to Wren Aske, who was a grown woman by then and had a husband and two children and a house above the flood line. She ferried for twenty years herself, until they built the bridge at Coldharbour and there was no more need of a ferry.

The boat is gone now. They say it was burned, or sunk, or broken up for firewood in the hard winter. The jetty at Reedhollow has rotted down to a row of black stumps in the water. And there is no ferryman on Mirrormere any more, to carry the drowned across.

The fishermen still say, though, that on the evening of the first frost, if you stand on the shore at Reedhollow and look out at the middle of the lake, you may see a lantern burning out on the water where no boat should be. And some of them say it burns blue, and some of them say it burns gold, and I have never been able to decide which I would rather it was.

As for me, I keep the old custom. When I lose someone, I put two coppers on the windowsill that faces the lake, and a silver penny if they died after dark. The lake keeps what it is given. I would like to think it still gives back what it is owed.
